## Suggested Links

U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) pages have the authoritative information on dog food recalls:

[**FDA Recalls & Withdrawals**](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/safety-health/recalls-withdrawals) is a searchable index of notices posted by the FDA but usually written by the company doing the recalling. There are typically several entries per month.

[**FDA Outbreaks and Advisories**](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/news-events/outbreaks-and-advisories) is a short list written by the FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine in its own voice. There are a few "do not feed these lots" notices per year, plus occasional longer investigations such as the one into certain diets and canine dilated cardiomyopathy. Advisories are updated in-place. For example, the January 2026 Raaw Energy advisory opened with 8 lots and listed more than 180 by May.

[**American Veterinary Medical Foundation (AVMF) Recalls & Safety Alerts**](https://www.avma.org/news/recalls-alerts) is worth having, but it isn't a third authority. It summarizes each FDA recall and links to the FDA source. You can follow their [@AVMARecallWatch](https://twitter.com/AVMARecallWatch) on social media for recall alerts.

## Know what to match

The most useful thing you can do about a dog food recall happens before a recall exists: keep the bag.

A recall notice can name a brand you feed and still not be about your dog's food. Photograph the back of the bag when you open it, before the label goes in the trash.

Capture **the lot or date code**, **the best-by date**, and of course **the brand and product name**. The latter gets you to the right notice and the other two are how you tell whether yours is one of the lots named in it.

## Work a recall notice

When a notice names a brand you feed, work it in this order:

1. Search the FDA Recalls & Withdrawals by brand or product name.
2. Compare the lot or date code and best-by date on your bag with the affected lots in the notice.
3. If the code matches, the FDA notice says what to do for your dog and sometimes gives a company contact number.
4. Check for an FDA Advisory, too. An advisory is updated in-place, so it may be where the latest information lives.

## Report, report, report

Reports are the main way problems like these get found!

If your dog becomes ill and you suspect the food, [the FDA explains how to report a
pet food complaint](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/report-problem/how-report-pet-food-complaint).
Reports go to the FDA's Safety Reporting Portal. The FDA says it reviews them for
follow-up where possible, and that even reports needing no testing still
contribute to the surveillance used to spot future problems.
